GoFundMe Pro · Fundraising Benchmarks

How is this organization really doing?

Every nonprofit raises a different amount for different reasons. This compares each one against organizations genuinely like it — same cause, same size — and turns the difference into a short list of what to try next.

The problem

"Raised $180,000" doesn't tell you anything

A $180k year is outstanding for a small animal rescue and disappointing for a hospital foundation. Every organization has different donors, a different cause, different staff, a different budget. "Good" only means something relative to organizations genuinely like you.

So this builds that comparison group, scores each organization inside it, and turns the shortfall into a ranked list of things to actually do.

The five steps

Step 1 — Agree what the words mean

Three ideas each had more than one answer in the data. Each had to be reduced to one before any analysis could run.

IdeaThe problemWhat was decided
Cause areaThe category label was written two different ways for the same category.Rebuild it from the official IRS code letter. Accounts with no code are excluded rather than pooled into a fake "unknown" group.
Organization sizeThree revenue fields disagreed. One was typed by hand: 86 accounts said exactly $10,000,000, 29 said exactly $1,000,000,000, 12 said exactly $1.Discard the hand-typed field. Use the figure from the organization's IRS filing.
"Raised"Some columns meant last 12 months, others everything since they joined. They look identical side by side.Judge performance on the last 12 months only. Anything all-time is labelled and never mixed in.
Why this gets its own step. Getting it wrong produces no error message — just confident, wrong answers. Comparing one organization's last 12 months against another's lifetime total would flatter whoever had been on the platform longest, and nothing in the output would look amiss.

Step 2 — Decide who can be measured

An organization can only be compared if we know its cause, its size and its results. Just over half the book was missing at least one.

The two big losses are fixable. 1,589 accounts have no IRS cause code and 901 have no filed revenue. Both are public information available by tax ID. Backfilling them would widen coverage more than any change to the analysis itself.

Step 3 — Build the peer groups

Each organization is grouped with others sharing its cause area and its revenue size. Size brackets step up in multiples, not fixed amounts, because fundraising capacity scales that way — $1M to $5M is a similar kind of leap as $100M to $500M.

A group needs at least 30 organizations. In a group of four, one unusual member drags everyone's score around. Where a cause-and-size cell is too small, the organization is compared against every organization of its size instead — giving up the cause match rather than reporting a number built on five peers.

A mistake worth showing. The largest bracket was originally just "over $25M" with no ceiling. That put NewYork-Presbyterian, at $6.7 billion in revenue, in the same peer group as $26M foundations — a 322-fold spread. Its scores computed correctly and were still misleading: it looked like it was raising 23× its peer median when the honest figure against organizations its own size is 6×. Brackets were split, and a test now fails if an open-ended one is ever reintroduced.

Step 4 — Score everyone against their group

Everyone gets a percentile. 50 is exactly mid-pack; 90 means only one in ten peers did better. Because the group is already matched on size, no further adjustment is needed — the comparison is size-fair by construction.

Not everyone at the bottom is underperforming

The very largest gaps belonged to organizations that had barely started using the platform — one raised $23,000 against a peer median of $777,000. That is not a fundraising problem; nothing has launched yet. 268 accounts are flagged for onboarding instead and get no lever advice.

Not everyone at the top needs advice

The biggest fundraiser in the book has a $26 average gift — near the bottom of its group — because it runs a mass-market model with 698,792 monthly donors. Told to raise that gift, the model projected +$413M: nonsense, and advice to abandon what works. 374 accounts get a scorecard but no recommendations.

Step 5 — Work out which actions matter

A model compares organizations within each peer group and asks which operating differences go with raising more. Five things an organization can actually change came out significant.

For one account, each lever is nudged from where it sits toward where its peers sit, and the model converts that into an expected change. Moves are capped at a realistic step — an early version advised jumping from the 5th to the 75th percentile in one go and projected "+2,560%".

Realistic potential

The idea underneath all of this. Rather than judging an organization against its own goal or a conversion rate — both of which move with donor base, marketing spend, staff and luck — realistic potential normalizes performance against a peer cohort to define a lower and an upper bound. Success is then how close an organization gets to that band.

TermDefinition
Realistic potentialThe performance band a peer cohort actually achieves — not a target, a range. Here, the 25th to 75th percentile of the cohort, with the median as the reference point.
SuccessHow close an organization gets to its realistic potential. Expressed as a percentile inside its cohort rather than as percentage-to-goal.
Peer cohortThe comparison set that makes the bounds meaningful: cause area × revenue size, minimum 30 organizations.
Why not "did they hit their goal?" Goals are set by the organization, so completion-to-goal measures ambition as much as performance — a cautious goal makes a weak year look excellent. Conversion rate has the same problem from the other direction: it moves with donor mix, marketing spend and staff capability, none of which are comparable across organizations. Normalizing to a peer cohort is what makes a bound defensible, and it is why the cohort definition gets as much attention here as the model does.
What this build implements, and what it does not. The bounds above are computed per organization, per year — 25th/75th percentile of annual dollars raised within the cohort. The fuller definition of realistic potential is per campaign, per campaign type, with success also read from momentum (week-over-week donations against cohort week-over-week) and the donation curve (growth and decay against cohort curves). None of that is possible from this export: it has no campaign records and no weekly time series, only annual and lifetime totals. The cohort machinery is the same either way — campaign-level data would drop into it without redesign.

What is and is not accounted for

Being explicit matters more than looking complete, so here is the boundary in full.

The most important gap: we only see the campaigns an organization runs with us. An organization that runs its peer-to-peer with GoFundMe Pro and its direct giving somewhere else shows a small number here and is not underperforming — most of its programme is invisible to this analysis. There is no wallet-share field in the data, so this cannot be corrected, only flagged.

How big is that effect? Measured, not assumed.

So roughly half of the apparent shortfall of a narrow-footprint organization is a measurement artifact — channels we never record — and roughly half is genuinely lower activity on the channels we do see. That is why the flag is a scope warning rather than a correction: the honest statement is "part of this gap is not real, and we cannot tell you which part for any single organization."

Who this affects. 1,004 of 3,161 accounts (32%) carry a narrow footprint, including 218 of the 484 "coachable gap" accounts (45%) — $25.2M of the $60.0M aggregate gap. Those accounts show a scope warning on their scorecard, and a seller should confirm what the organization runs elsewhere before treating any shortfall as real. It also makes "add a fundraising channel" a partly circular recommendation: it may be advising an organization to move a programme it deliberately runs elsewhere.

What to trust, and what not to

Trust these

  • Percentile position — where an organization sits among genuinely comparable peers.
  • The order of the actions — which lever is likeliest to matter most.
  • The three-way split — whether this is an onboarding, coaching, or leave-well-alone conversation.
  • Who the peers are — the full comparison set is listed by name.

Don't trust these

  • Exact dollar projections — individually off by roughly a factor of two. Never a target.
  • Cause and effect — organizations with a CRM raise more, but the kind that installs one was often already ahead.
  • "Should be at the median" — about 80% of the variation is inside peer groups, not between them.
  • Campaign-level questions — the data is per organization; there are no individual campaign records.
In one line: this tells you where an organization stands against real peers and gives a sensibly ordered list of what to try. It does not tell you what will happen if they do it.

01  Fundraising is extraordinarily concentrated

Share of all trailing-year dollars raised, by the top slice of accounts. The top 1% raise nearly half of everything.

What follows from it. A flat per-account coverage model wastes effort. Half the money sits with about 30 organizations, and the median account raises $122,000 — those two facts call for different playbooks, not one.

02  Almost everyone scales on volume, not gift size

Of the 317 accounts in the top tenth of fundraisers, how did they get there — a bigger average gift, or more gifts?

The most useful single finding here. Volume-led outnumbers gift-led 13 to 1. Growing the number of donors — especially recurring ones — is how nearly every top performer got there, and it matches what the model finds independently: recurring donors is the most robust lever of the five. When in doubt, that is the advice.

03  One channel carries the platform

Lifetime dollars raised by channel, all accounts.

Note the mismatch. Campaign Studio is live on the most accounts (3,639) but raises a fifth of what donation pages do. Registration is barely used at all — 233 accounts, $9M. Adoption breadth and dollar contribution are not the same thing, and a "more channels" recommendation should be read with that in mind.

04  Matched peers still differ enormously

Within the largest peer groups — already matched on both cause and size — the 10th to 90th percentile range of trailing-year raised.

10th–90th percentilemedian
Read percentiles, not targets. In Human Services · $1M–$5M the 10th percentile raises $18k and the 90th raises $562k — a 30× spread among organizations of the same cause and size. Cohorting makes the comparison fair; it does not make peers alike. "You should be at the median" is not a claim this data supports.

05  Some causes produce far more breakaway leaders

Share of each sector's accounts raising at least 5× their own peer-group median. Because the comparison is within-cohort, this is not a size effect.

Where the playbooks are. Religion Related produces standouts at 2–3× the rate of most sectors — congregational giving concentrates in a way other causes do not. Those are the accounts worth interviewing for what they do differently.

06  What actually moves fundraising

Effect of each lever on annual raised, holding peer group, organization scale and fundraising history constant.

Association, not proof. These come from comparing organizations, not from running an experiment. Organizations that already fundraise well are likelier to have installed a CRM in the first place, so each number blends "this helps" with "the kind of organization that does this was already ahead."
