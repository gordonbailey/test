The problem

"Raised $180,000" doesn't tell you anything

$180k is a brilliant year for a small animal rescue and a poor one for a hospital foundation. "Good" only means something next to organizations genuinely like you.

So this builds that comparison group, scores each organization inside it, and turns the shortfall into a short list of things to try.

The five steps

Step 1 — Agree what the words mean

Three things each had more than one answer in the data. Each had to be settled before anything else could run.

Three definitions that each had more than one answer
IdeaThe problemWhat was decided
Cause areaThe category label was written two different ways for the same category.Rebuild it from the official IRS code. Accounts with no code are left out rather than dumped into a fake "unknown" group.
Organization sizeThree revenue fields disagreed. One was typed by hand: 86 accounts said exactly $10,000,000, 29 said exactly $1,000,000,000, 12 said exactly $1.Throw away the hand-typed field. Use the figure from the IRS filing.
"Raised"Some columns meant last 12 months, others everything since they joined. They look identical side by side.Judge everyone on the last 12 months only. Anything all-time is labeled and never mixed in.
Why this gets its own step. Getting it wrong produces no error message, just confident wrong answers. Comparing one organization's last 12 months against another's lifetime total would flatter whoever had been here longest, and nothing on screen would look wrong.

Step 2 — Decide who can be measured

You can only compare an organization if you know its cause, its size and its results. Just over half were missing at least one.

The two big losses are fixable. 1,589 accounts have no cause code and 901 have no revenue figure. Both are public information you can look up by tax ID. Filling them in would widen coverage more than any change to the analysis.

Step 3 — Build the peer groups

Each organization is grouped with others of the same cause and the same size. The size brackets multiply rather than add, because fundraising scales that way: $1M to $5M is a similar leap to $100M to $500M.

A group needs at least 30 organizations. In a group of four, one odd member drags everyone's score around. Where there are too few of a given cause and size, the organization is compared against everyone of its size instead — giving up the cause match rather than reporting a number built on five peers.

A mistake worth showing. The top bracket was originally "over $25M" with no ceiling, which put NewYork-Presbyterian at $6.7 billion in the same group as $26M foundations. The maths was right and the answer was misleading: it looked like it was raising 23× its peers when the honest figure against organizations its own size is 6×. The brackets were split, and a test now fails if anyone reintroduces an open-ended one.

Step 4 — Score everyone against their group

Everyone gets a percentile. 50 is exactly mid-pack; 90 means only one peer in ten did better. Because the group is already matched on size, nothing further needs adjusting.

Not everyone at the bottom is underperforming

The biggest gaps belonged to organizations that had barely started using the platform. One raised $23,000 against a peer median of $777,000. That is not a fundraising problem — nothing has launched yet. 268 accounts are flagged for onboarding instead, and get no advice.

Not everyone at the top needs advice

The biggest fundraiser here has a $26 average gift, near the bottom of its group, because it runs a mass-market model with 698,792 monthly donors. Told to raise that average, the model predicted +$413M — nonsense, and advice to abandon the thing that works. 374 accounts get a scorecard and no recommendations.

Step 5 — Work out which actions matter

A model compares organizations inside each peer group and asks what the ones raising more do differently. Five things an organization can actually change came out as mattering.

For one account, each of the five is nudged from where it sits toward where its peers sit, and the model works out what that would be worth. The nudges are capped at something realistic — an early version suggested jumping from the 5th to the 75th percentile in one leap and predicted "+2,560%".

Realistic potential

The idea underneath all of this. Rather than judging an organization against its own goal or a conversion rate — both of which move with donor base, marketing spend, staff and luck — realistic potential normalizes performance against a peer cohort to define a lower and an upper bound. Success is then how close an organization gets to that band.

TermDefinition
Realistic potentialThe performance band a peer cohort actually achieves — not a target, a range. Here, the 25th to 75th percentile of the cohort, with the median as the reference point.
SuccessHow close an organization gets to its realistic potential. Expressed as a percentile inside its cohort rather than as percentage-to-goal.
Peer cohortThe comparison set that makes the bounds meaningful: cause area × revenue size, minimum 30 organizations.
Why not "did they hit their goal?" Goals are set by the organization, so completion-to-goal measures ambition as much as performance — a cautious goal makes a weak year look excellent. Conversion rate has the same problem from the other direction: it moves with donor mix, marketing spend and staff capability, none of which are comparable across organizations. Normalizing to a peer cohort is what makes a bound defensible, and it is why the cohort definition gets as much attention here as the model does.
What this build implements, and what it does not. The bounds above are computed per organization, per year — 25th/75th percentile of annual dollars raised within the cohort. The fuller definition of realistic potential is per campaign, per campaign type, with success also read from momentum (week-over-week donations against cohort week-over-week) and the donation curve (growth and decay against cohort curves). None of that is possible from this export: it has no campaign records and no weekly time series, only annual and lifetime totals. The cohort machinery is the same either way — campaign-level data would drop into it without redesign.

What this can and cannot see

Being clear about the edges matters more than looking complete.

The biggest gap: we only see the fundraising they do with us. An organization that runs its events here and its everyday giving somewhere else looks small in this data and is not underperforming — most of what they do is invisible. Nothing in the data says how much of their fundraising we handle, so this can be flagged but never corrected.

Who this affects. 1,149 of 3,161 accounts do only one kind of fundraising with us, including 195 of the 484 accounts we would otherwise coach — $22.5M of the $60.0M total gap. Those accounts carry a warning on their scorecard. Ask what they run elsewhere before treating any shortfall as real.

What to trust, and what not to

Trust these

  • Where they rank among genuinely comparable peers.
  • The order of the suggestions — which one is likeliest to matter most.
  • Which conversation to have — onboarding, coaching, or leave them alone.
  • Who the peers are — every one is listed by name.

Don't trust these

  • The exact dollar figures — individually off by around a factor of two. Never use one as a target.
  • Cause and effect — organizations with better tools raise more, but the kind that buys them was often already ahead.
  • "They should be at the median" — most of the variation is inside peer groups, not between them.
  • Anything about a single campaign — this data is per organization, not per campaign.
In one line: this tells you where an organization stands against real peers, and what to try first. It does not tell you what will happen if they do it.

01  Fundraising is extraordinarily concentrated

Share of everything raised last year, by the top slice of accounts.

What follows. Treating every account the same wastes effort. Half the money sits with about 30 organizations, and the typical account raises $122,000. Those two need different playbooks.

02  Almost everyone scales on volume, not gift size

Of the 317 accounts in the top tenth, how did they get there — bigger gifts, or more of them?

The most useful finding on this page. More donors beats bigger gifts, 13 to 1. Growing the number of donors, especially monthly ones, is how nearly every top performer got there — and the model finds the same thing on its own. When in doubt, that is the advice.

03  Direct giving is three quarters of everything

All-time dollars by kind of fundraising. Donation Page and Campaign Studio are not two different things — one is a page, the other is a way to build that page. Our reporting splits them, which makes organizations look like they do more kinds of fundraising than they really do.

Why fixing this mattered. 60% of accounts have money in both, with Studio typically 13% of their direct giving — the signature of a page builder, not a separate channel. Rolling seven report columns into three real kinds of fundraising made the model better, and it changed what the advice means: "diversify" now means try a kind of fundraising you do not do with us, not use a second page builder.
That grouping is my inference, not documentation. I worked it out from the column names, not from a product spec. It is one line of code to change if someone who knows tells me it is wrong. Worth confirming.

04  Matched peers still differ enormously

Inside the biggest peer groups — already matched on cause and size — how far apart the members still are.

10th–90th percentilemedian
Read the ranking, not a target. In Human Services at $1M–$5M, the bottom end raises $18k and the top end raises $562k — thirty times apart, among organizations of the same cause and the same size. Matching peers makes the comparison fair. It does not make them alike. "You should be at the median" is not something this data supports.

05  Some causes produce far more breakaway leaders

Share of each cause area raising at least 5× their own peer group's median. Since everyone is compared within their own group, this is not just big organizations.

Where the playbooks are. Religious organizations produce standouts at two to three times the rate of other causes. Congregational giving concentrates in a way nothing else does. Those are the accounts worth interviewing.

Organizations that already fundraise well are more likely to have bought good tools in the first place, so every number here mixes "this helps" with "the kind of organization that does this was already ahead".

Three things to hold onto.

1. The "behind" column holds out organizations that run only part of their fundraising through the platform, and those that have barely launched. Their gap is scope or onboarding, not performance.
2. The very largest multiples are partly an artifact. Size bands come from the organization's IRS filing, and 84 accounts raised more on the platform in twelve months than their whole filed revenue — so the filing understates them, the peer group is too small, and the multiple is too big. Tunnel to Towers at 903× is a banding problem, not a fundraising fact.
3. Nobody agreed to be named a bottom performer. Publish the pattern, never the ranking.
