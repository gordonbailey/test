Why these exist

Everything else in this portfolio is analysis — a question asked and answered. These two are different: they are attempts to make something reusable by someone who was not here. An analysis ages into a historical record. A guide either stays useful or it does not, and that depends almost entirely on whether it is honest about how much it knows.

The test each one has to pass: could a new product manager, on their first week, get a useful answer out of this without asking anybody — and without being confidently misled?

The thing both have to get right

State your confidence, per claim, on the page

The repo guide's most valuable feature is not the map — it is that ownership is labelled documented, inferred or contested, and that 271 repos it could not classify are shown as unclassified rather than forced into a cluster. A guide that hides its uncertainty gets trusted during an incident and sends someone to the wrong place. One bad answer costs more credibility than ten missing ones.

Teach the question, not just the answer

Both guides are built so the reader leaves able to ask something useful. The non-functional guide gives every requirement the questions to ask engineering and the spec language to write down; the repo guide's wayfinder walks you down the path a patient senior engineer would. Neither tries to make the reader an expert — they try to make the next conversation a competent one, which is a much more achievable goal.

A guide with no home is not a guide

Both are single self-contained HTML files, which is exactly why they work offline and exactly why they disappear at handover. The same risk showed up in the UTM tickets, where one referenced Confluence page sits in a personal space. Hosting is not an afterthought here — for a handover artifact it is most of the value, and it is the first action on both pages.
