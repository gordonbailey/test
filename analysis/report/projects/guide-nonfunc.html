The problem

A working feature answers "what does it do?" Everything that decides whether it holds up in the real world — speed, reliability, safety, and how expensive it will be to change later — sits in a vocabulary most product managers were never taught.

Without those words, the conversation goes one of two ways. The product manager nods along and finds out what "no kill switch" meant during an outage. Or they avoid the subject entirely, and the engineer becomes the only person who can judge whether a release is safe.

This is a translation problem. The concepts are well documented — in engineering books, for engineers. Almost nothing puts them in a form a product manager can use. So every one of the 24 entries gets the same four things: a plain definition, what good looks like, the questions to ask engineering, and the wording to put in the spec. The goal is a reader who can ask one question that changes the design.

What was done

Five sections, built in the order a product manager needs them. Product Dev puts the vocabulary inside the process they already work in. Architecture follows one click through the whole system, so each requirement attaches to a real place rather than sitting in a list. Requirements is the reference layer, meant to be opened while writing a spec. Then flashcards, a quiz, and twelve things worth reading.

All of it is running in the frame above.

What came out of it

The six to learn first

Half of them are about running the thing rather than building it. The requirements most likely to cause an outage are the ones least likely to appear in a spec.

RequirementCategoryIn one line
LatencyPerformanceHow long the user waits between an action and a useful response. Felt, not averaged: percentile targets for specific actions, measured end to end including network.
ObservabilityOpsUnderstanding what the system is doing, and why, from the outside without guessing. The test is whether it answers questions nobody anticipated.
AlertsOpsSignals that tell a human something needs attention before users notice. Fire on user-impacting symptoms, each one actionable, owned and linked to a runbook.
RolloutsOpsHow a change reaches users: all at once, or gradually with the ability to stop and reverse. Releasing decoupled from deploying.
Test CoverageQualityHow much behavior is verified automatically, and which behavior. Critical paths tested at the right level, in a suite people trust.
SimplicityQualitySolving the problem with the least complexity that works. No speculative machinery for requirements that may never come.

The rest of it

The other 18 cover reliability, security, data, accessibility and cost. There is also a 53-item launch checklist split across three moments — at design, before release, and after — plus a rotating Release Lead role so somebody owns the checklist rather than everybody assuming somebody did. Open the guide above for any of it.

What it does not cover

It teaches the question, not GoFundMe's answer

The guide prompts "what is our target for this?" without answering it. Our actual speed and reliability targets are out of scope, so a reader finishes able to ask and still unable to answer.

Nothing here is enforced

A 53-item checklist and a rotating role stay a suggestion until they are attached to a ticket template or a definition of done. Right now it depends on somebody choosing to open the file.

The 24 are not ranked for a donation platform

A failed donation is not a retryable inconvenience. Data integrity and compliance almost certainly matter more here than the generic ordering suggests, and the guide does not make that case.

The quiz tests recall, and nothing tests use

Whether someone who took it writes better specs is unmeasured. Nothing saves between sessions, so there is no way to tell who has been through it or where people get stuck.
