The problem

Without that vocabulary the conversation goes one of two ways. The PM nods along and finds out what "no kill switch" meant during an incident, or the PM avoids the topic and the engineer becomes the only person who can assess release risk.

This is a translation problem. The concepts are well documented in SRE literature written for engineers, and almost nowhere in a form a PM can act on.

So the guide is a translation layer. Every one of the 24 requirements gets the same four things: a plain definition, what good looks like, the questions to ask engineering, and the spec language for a PRD, plus the tradeoff to reason about. The goal is a reader who can ask a question that changes the design.

Progressive rollout, for comparison: deploy dark with the flag off in prod → 1% canary → 10% watching error rate → 50% watching conversion → 100%. A health gate at each stage, a kill switch that reverts in under 30 seconds with no redeploy, and the error budget governing the pace. Each stage names the metric being watched.

What came out of it

The six that come first

Three of the six are operational. The requirements most likely to cause an outage are the ones least likely to appear in a PRD.

RequirementCategoryIn one line
LatencyPerformanceHow long the user waits between an action and a useful response. Felt, not averaged: percentile targets for specific actions, measured end to end including network.
ObservabilityOpsUnderstanding what the system is doing, and why, from the outside without guessing. The test is whether it answers questions nobody anticipated.
AlertsOpsSignals that tell a human something needs attention before users notice. Fire on user-impacting symptoms, each one actionable, owned and linked to a runbook.
RolloutsOpsHow a change reaches users: all at once, or gradually with the ability to stop and reverse. Releasing decoupled from deploying.
Test CoverageQualityHow much behaviour is verified automatically, and which behaviour. Critical paths tested at the right level, in a suite people trust.
SimplicityQualitySolving the problem with the least complexity that works. No speculative machinery for requirements that may never come.

The release-readiness process: 53 items, three phases

A checklist is only useful if it fires at the right moment, so each phase has a stated time and purpose.

Build · 27 items

At design / kickoff

Attached to the epic when work begins. Nine groups:

  • Architecture & Dependencies
  • Database Changes
  • API Design
  • New Queues / Async Pipelines
  • Tests
  • Feature Flags
  • Observability
  • Timeouts & Resilience
  • Broad Infra / Platform-Wide
Pre-Release · 13 items

Hours before deploy

Explicitly framed as "a quick confirm, not a discovery" — which is the whole reason the Build checklist exists earlier. Finding a missing dependency at this point is already a failure.

Post-Release · 13 items

First 30–60 minutes

Ten verification items, plus three that only apply if a rollback is triggered — so the rollback path is pre-written rather than improvised under pressure.

The Release Lead

A rotating role aimed at a named class of failure: incomplete blast-radius assessments, missing alerting on new workflows, config that only breaks in prod, and broad changes with no kill switch. The Release Lead may delegate the checks but owns the release end to end.

  • Runs all three checklists and gets every item signed off.
  • Pulls in affected teams early rather than discovering dependencies at pre-release.
  • Owns the go / no-go and rollback decisions.
  • Available for the first hour post-release, monitoring key signals, and writes a short summary if anything goes sideways.
  • Documents gaps so the checklist improves over time.
Documenting the gaps is what keeps the checklist current. Several items already read as though they came out of a postmortem: "worker count × polling loops ≤ queue rate limit at restart (e.g. 160 loops vs a ~300 req/s cap)" is a specific incident, not a generic best practice. So is "alerts that were never actually wired up are their own kind of outage."

The process it sits inside

Product Dev traces the cycle: inputs (sales, customers, competitors, user research, product analytics) → discovery, iterating with the PRD → solution design and prototyping → an optional spike → epic, story, task, bug and sub-tasks. Four principles sit underneath:

Problems before solutionsStart with customers, then the business, and only then solutions. Weigh desirability, feasibility and viability.
The triad steers each squadProduct Manager, Engineering Manager and Designer share the wheel.
Planning is bottoms-up and tops-downContext → Plans → Integration → Buy-in. Leadership sets direction, teams shape the work, they meet in the middle.
Balance the portfolioHealthy roadmaps mix stability, improvements, growth and bets. Every quarter: do we like the mix, and are we still on the right things?

The PDLC framing is honest about itself: stages overlap, research runs throughout, and "complete" means "complete enough to move on."

What it does not cover

It is a vocabulary and a process, not GoFundMe's numbers

The guide prompts the question "what is our p95 target for this action?" without answering it. GoFundMe's actual latency targets, SLOs, error budgets and availability commitments are out of scope, so a PM finishes it able to ask and still unable to answer.

Nothing here is enforced

53 checklist items and a Release Lead role stay a proposal until they attach to a Jira template, a definition of done, or a required epic field. As written it depends on someone choosing to open the file.

The 24 are not weighted for this product

A donation platform has an unusual risk profile: payments correctness, PCI scope, giving-season load spikes, and a failed donation that is not a retryable inconvenience. Data Integrity and Compliance probably rank higher here than the generic ordering suggests, and the guide does not make that case.

No accessibility depth, despite the audience

Accessibility appears as one of 24 requirements alongside internationalization and compatibility. For a public donation product it is both a legal exposure and a conversion issue, and it would justify more than parity with cost efficiency.

The quiz measures recall, and nothing measures use

Flashcards and a quiz test whether the vocabulary stuck. Whether a PM who took it writes better PRDs is unmeasured, and since nothing saves between sessions there is no way to tell who has been through it or where people get stuck.

It still has no internal URL

Embedding it here gives it a home, and the source is in the repo, but neither is an internal link a Jira epic template or a PRD template can point at. That is the remaining step.
