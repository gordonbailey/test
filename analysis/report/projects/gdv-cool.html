The problem

Giving on GoFundMe is episodic. Donors arrive for a crisis and disappear, and there is no identity, status or habit attached to being generous here. Marketplace is a first attempt at an answer, but a donation is not inherently cool — the experience around giving is where coolness could live.

Which raises the real question: coolness has to be borrowed from somewhere. From where? The intuitive answer is fame — athletes, celebrities, musicians, creators. The data points somewhere else.

What was done

Two hypotheses were available to test against the referral data on donation events, using second-gift rate within 90 days as the proxy for whether a donor formed a habit:

HypothesisWhat it predictsVerdict
Borrowed fame
celebrities, athletes, creators		Donors arriving through broadcast social channels should form habits at least as well as anyone else — reach creates culture.Not supported
Giving is social
"with people, not alone on your phone"		Donors arriving through a person — a shared link, a message — should return at higher rates than donors who arrive alone.Supported

The split is interpersonal versus broadcast — not social versus non-social. Someone who arrived through a link a person handed them (chat, copy-link, email, referral) returns at 19–28%. Someone who arrived through organic social returns at 10.6% — below the platform average of 12.3%. Broadcast social is the worst-retaining channel in the table, and it is the channel a celebrity strategy buys.
So the recommendation inverts. "Borrow coolness from athletes, celebrities, creators" is a broadcast play, and broadcast produces the least habit-forming donors measured here. "Make giving something you do with people rather than alone on your phone" is the interpersonal play, and it is 2.2× baseline. Giving circles are the productised version of the thing that already works — they are not a nice-to-have alongside the celebrity idea, they are the idea the evidence supports.

The productised answer: in-person community events

If the highest-retaining channels are all interpersonal, then the most interpersonal thing available is putting people in a room together. Three truths sit underneath this, and none of them is about giving.

Truth 1

People want to spend time together, in person

Not on a feed. Giving today is a solitary act performed on a phone — the least social possible expression of a fundamentally social impulse. That is the mismatch the whole strategy is circling.

Truth 2

People want to have fun

A run, a dinner, a show, a match, a pub quiz. The event carries the enjoyment and the giving rides along with it, rather than the giving being the ask. Nobody attends a fundraiser for fun; plenty of people attend a race that happens to raise money.

Truth 3

People want meaning and purpose

This is the one GoFundMe already owns outright. No other events business starts with it — Eventbrite, Strava and Meetup all have to manufacture meaning. Here it is the default. That is the unfair advantage, and it is currently unused.

Frame them as community events, not fundraisers — and capture the donation in the room. The framing matters because "fundraiser" makes the donation the price of entry, which is the opposite of how the retaining channels work. The donation should be the thing that happens because you were there.

The enabling capability is already built

In-person capture is not a new bet. Tap to Donate came out of the hackathon as the top-ranked build, and it exists to solve exactly this:

DemandMost-voted request in the book — 204+ customers, open since December 2014.
Named wedgeThe Salvation Army's ~$100M offline year-end campaign. QR codes already failed here on friction.
EffortTwo sprints, one person, per the lead engineer. No hardware — Apple and Google tap-to-pay inside the Live Events app.
CaveatThat estimate excludes PCI and Tap-to-Pay certification, which is the real gate.

The two projects need each other: events give the capability a reason to exist beyond one enterprise account, and the capability makes the events measurable. See the hackathon assessment for the full scope and estimate.

What would make this falsifiable. One instrumented pilot event — capture donations in the room via Tap to Donate, tag the attendees, and measure their 90-day second-gift rate against the 12.3% baseline and against the 27% copy-link cohort. If in-person attendance produces copy-link-grade retention, that is the strongest possible evidence for the whole interpersonal thesis. If it produces baseline retention, the thesis is about relationships rather than proximity, and the strategy changes.

What this does and does not license you to say

Fair to say

Donors who arrive through an interpersonal channel are roughly twice as likely to give again within 90 days as the platform average, and roughly 2.6× as likely as donors arriving via organic social. Person-to-person sharing is where repeat giving concentrates.

Not fair to say

That adding share buttons, or running a giving-circles feature, will produce a 2× lift. The channel is very likely a marker of relationship strength rather than a cause of it — a person who receives a link from a friend is already closer to the cause than a stranger scrolling a feed. The correlation is real; the causal arrow is unestablished.

What it does not cover

Selection, not causation — and it is the whole ballgame

The best-performing channels are the ones a friend uses to reach you. Those donors would plausibly have retained better regardless of the mechanism they arrived through. Nothing here separates "interpersonal channels build habits" from "people with existing relationships use interpersonal channels." An experiment that randomises the prompt to share, not the channel, is the only way through.

The best cells are the smallest

chat is n=699 and organic_social is n=1,053, against a 20.2M baseline — a few thousandths of a percent of donors. Only social (128,844), partner (76,659), email (69,867), referral (34,995) and the two large buckets carry enough volume to plan against. The headline 28.3% is directional colour, not a forecastable rate.

41% of donors have no medium at all

8.3M (none) plus 19,468 undefined out of 20.2M. So the entire comparison is drawn from the tagged 59%, and tagged traffic is not a random sample of all traffic — it skews toward campaigns someone bothered to instrument. This is the consumer-side view of the same attribution failure the UTM project exists to fix and the interview research found on the nonprofit side, where 96% of Salvation Army transactions carry no referral data.

The medium field is dirty enough to affect the read

Meta ad-set identifiers are leaking into campaign_medium — values like [Worldwide]|120239607932490314 and [C1] [EUA EU]|120239963371910207 appear as media. Email and email are separate values. customer,customer and customer,social are concatenations rather than single media. Some of what is labelled broadcast may be mislabelled, and vice versa.

Repeat gift is a proxy for "cool", and a narrow one

Second-gift rate measures habit formation. It says nothing about whether giving became identity-forming or socially visible, which is what the strategy is actually reaching for. A donor could return twice a year and still feel nothing about being a generous person.

No brand or attitudinal measurement at all

Everything here is behavioural. Whether giving is perceived as cool — by whom, relative to what — has not been asked. There is no survey, no brand tracker, and no segmentation of the audience the strategy is aimed at.

Pick this up next

1
Instrument sharing before building anything
Copy-link is the best-performing real-volume channel and it is barely measured — 13,985 tagged donors when the true number is certainly far higher. You cannot optimise a mechanism you cannot see.
2
Run the giving-circles test as an experiment, not a launch
Randomise the invitation to give alongside someone, hold out a control, and measure second-gift rate at 90 days. That converts a 2× correlation into a treatment effect — or kills it cheaply.
3
Separate the two jobs celebrities and friends do
Broadcast may still be the right instrument for first-gift volume; it is demonstrably the wrong one for habit. Fund them against different metrics rather than arguing about which is "the" strategy.
4
Add an attitudinal measure
If the goal is that giving feels cool, behaviour alone will never confirm it. One question in an existing survey — would you tell a friend you did this — gets further than another funnel query.
