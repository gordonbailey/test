The problem

Sixteen projects came out of the hackathon, each with an owner who believed in it. There was no way to compare a payments feature against a reliability fix against an AI reporting tool, and only a handful can be built. So: which ones earn a slot, and what has to be true first?

The easy mistakes are funding whatever demoed best and parking whatever had nobody in the room. Both end up ranking presentations instead of projects.

What was done

1
Score all 16 on the same three things
Money raised, customers kept, and how hard it is to build
2
Merge the duplicates before ranking
Two pairs of projects were really one project each. On its own, Comparative Insights looked like a nice-to-have; combined, it became the reason to build the Reporting Hub at all.
3
Get an effort estimate from whoever wrote the code
How long it would take, in their words, caveats included
4
Check each one against the 2026 company priorities
A project that fits none of them has nobody to sponsor it
5
Tier into build, validate and park
Every parked project says what would bring it back

Three things showed up again and again

Ideas kept merging

Overlapping projects kept resolving into one better one. Two pairs became two projects.

Everyone built insights

Most ideas circled the same job: help customers decide something from their own data.

The best cases were defensive

The two strongest projects protect money we already have rather than winning new money.

What it does not cover

The money figures are estimates, not forecasts

Low, middle and high cases, built so sixteen projects could be compared on one scale. None has been through finance, and the high cases assume take-up nobody has measured.

The timings leave out what actually gates a launch

Tap to Donate's month of work excludes payment-security certification. Live Events' month is real, but the team is booked until December.

Three projects have no usable estimate

One owner said so himself, one team never replied, and one needs investigation first. Ranking them next to costed projects makes them look more certain than they are.

Nobody asked a customer to pay for any of it

The demand evidence is interviews and votes. That shows want, not willingness to pay or to run it.

Two figures in the deck contradicted themselves

Smart Retry's headline said $6M a year against a table showing $1.5M; the table was right. Tap to Donate's upside read $31M in one place and $30M in another. This page carries the corrected numbers.
