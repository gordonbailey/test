The problem

"Acquire better donors" is not actionable until better has a definition. It could mean larger gifts, higher retention, more referrals, or greater lifetime value — and those point at different acquisition channels and different creative.

The definition this analysis adopts: a better donor is one who gives again. That follows from the GDV concentration work — a second gift takes a donor from $94.63 to $179.00 of annual value, and frequency, not gift size, is what separates the bands. It also has the advantage of being measurable within 90 days rather than requiring a year to evaluate.

Note the tension this creates with a gift-size definition: average gift falls as frequency rises, from $94.63 at one gift to $77.27 at five. Optimising for return visits and optimising for gift size are not the same objective, and a strategy has to pick.

What was done

A two-step funnel on eCommerce - Purchase → eCommerce - Purchase with a 90-day conversion window, over the last 6 months of consumer donation events, measuring what share of donors give again and how long it takes.

One earlier attempt was discarded rather than reported. A retention query returned a first-period figure of 2.6%, which cannot be reconciled with the frequency data — roughly 20% of annual donors give 2+ times, so a 2.6% repeat rate is not credible. Rather than publish a number that failed a sanity check, the construct was replaced with the funnel, whose 12.3% does reconcile with the annual figure. The retention result is excluded, and this note is here so nobody rediscovers it and assumes it was overlooked.

What came out of it

The window is three weeks

23
Median days from a gift to the next gift. Mean 29 days. Of donors who give again inside 90 days, half have done it within a little over three weeks.
The decision to come back is made in the first month, not the eleventh.
  • 12.3% of donors give again within 90 days — 2.49M of 20.2M. That is the baseline any programme has to beat.
  • It reconciles with the annual picture: ~20% of donors reach 2+ gifts over 365 days, so 12.3% at 90 days is the right order of magnitude.
This retires one of the proposed mechanics as a conversion tool. An annual giving recap — Giving Wrapped — lands in December for a decision that was made in February. It is a legitimate identity and retention play for donors who already came back, and it may well be worth building. It is not a 1→2 conversion lever, and funding it as one would be a category error. The conversion lever is a three-week post-donation window.

What a point of lift is worth

Each additional second gift is worth about $89.50 — the average gift in the 2-gift band. Across roughly 40 million donor-anchors a year, one percentage point on the 90-day repeat rate is worth ~$36M annually.

Treat that as an order of magnitude, not a forecast. It counts donor-anchors rather than unique donors, so a frequent donor is counted more than once; and it assumes an incremental gift is worth the band average. It is the right size of number for prioritisation and the wrong one for a business case.

What it does not cover

The funnel measures gift → next gift, not strictly first → second

Step one is any gift, so a donor with six gifts contributes multiple anchors. The 12.3% therefore sits slightly above the true first-to-second conversion rate. Isolating genuine first gifts needs an nth-time filter that this analysis did not construct.

90 days is a ceiling imposed by the tool, not the biology

Amplitude funnels cap the conversion window at 90 days. Donors who return at 4–12 months are invisible here, and the annual data says they exist — 20% reach 2+ gifts over 365 days against 12.3% at 90. So roughly a third of eventual repeat giving happens outside the window this analysis can see.

Channel comparisons rest on the tagged 59%

With 41% of donors carrying no medium, and the medium field polluted by Meta ad-set IDs and case duplicates, the channel table describes instrumented traffic rather than all traffic. Any acquisition decision taken from it inherits that bias.

"Better" excludes the major-gift donor by construction

A donor who gives $5,000 once is not a better donor under this definition, and is not in the super donor cohort. If the company's growth answer includes major giving, this framing does not reach it — and the falling average gift size with frequency suggests the two populations may be genuinely different people.

No acquisition cost anywhere in this

Repeat rate by channel says nothing about what each channel costs to acquire from. A channel converting at 20% at four times the CPA is worse than one converting at 12%. The ROI comparison — the one an acquisition decision actually needs — is not possible from this data.

Nothing here is causal

Same limit as everywhere else in this branch: these are observed differences between populations, not measured effects of any intervention.

Pick this up next

1
Build the three-week intervention and hold out a control
The window is known and the baseline is known — 12.3% at 90 days. That is everything needed to run a clean test, and a holdout is what turns the $36M-per-point figure into something defensible.
2
Get acquisition cost next to repeat rate
Without CPA, the channel table ranks channels on half the equation. This is the single biggest gap between this analysis and an acquisition decision.
3
Extend the window past 90 days
A third of eventual repeat giving happens outside what the funnel can see. A cohort query in the warehouse rather than Amplitude would close that.
4
Decide whether frequency or gift size is the objective
They trade off — average gift falls from $94.63 to $77.27 as frequency rises. Picking one is a strategy decision, not an analytics one, and it should be made explicitly rather than by whichever KPI someone happens to report.
