The problem, and the answer

All three strands attack the same question: how do we tell a customer something true about their own fundraising that they could not work out on their own?

The problem

A number with no reference point

A nonprofit sees its conversion rate, its total raised, its average gift, and cannot tell whether any of it is good. City of Hope asked exactly that in an interview: is this a good conversion rate? Without an answer, an account manager has an opinion rather than a recommendation, and every renewal conversation starts from scratch.

What is needed

A comparison that survives scrutiny

Platform averages do not survive contact with a customer: a $2M food bank is not comparable to a $200M hospital foundation. The comparison has to hold cause and size constant, and it has to end in something the organization can do rather than a percentile it can only feel bad about.

The answer

Realistic potential, through peer cohorts

Group every eligible account with organizations genuinely like it, score it inside that group, and convert the shortfall into a ranked list of levers with an expected dollar lift on each. The output is not "you are behind" but "here is the realistic ceiling for an organization like you, and the two moves that close most of the distance".

Built and working: 3,161 accounts across 38 peer groups, with 73% of the variation in funds raised explained inside those groups. Every account gets a percentile, a coachable gap in dollars, and up to three ranked levers — plus a scorecard PDF a seller can hand to the customer. See the fundraising benchmarking engine.

Where they meet

The three ran separately and landed on the same gap.

Customers asked for benchmarks by name — and the market does not supply them

City of Hope sees a conversion rate with no context and asks whether it is good. Meanwhile peer benchmarking exists in the market in four shapes — consultant cohorts, annual studies, quarterly reports, and one unverifiable in-product claim — but no vendor evidences real-time, per-org comparison inside the workflow, and none has an answer for an organization sitting below its peers. The benchmarking engine was built against that gap.

"Prove it worked" is both the customer ask and the market's blind spot

Vine Maple Place turned Intelligent Ask on and could not tell whether it helped. Cedars-Sinai had its worst Giving Tuesday after two simultaneous changes and could not attribute it. Independently, all nine competitors publish trust and explainability language, and not one offers lift an organization can check in its own numbers.

Attribution is where the customer pain becomes an engineering problem

96% of Salvation Army transactions carry no referral data. FMSC cannot export the UTM parameters in its own links. Source codes drop during P2P setup and at site boundaries. That is the customer-visible symptom of what the Instrumentation and UTM projects address — which is why those two sit outside this group but belong to the same argument.

Read these together with care. The interviews are qualitative and recruited, the competitive scan is vendor marketing rather than verified behavior, and the benchmarking engine runs on a single CRM export snapshot. Three sources with three different weaknesses agreeing is encouraging, not proof. Each page carries its own limits under what it does not cover.
