Where they meet

The three were run separately and converged anyway, which is the most useful thing about them. Two independent lines of evidence landing on the same gap is worth more than either one alone.

Customers asked for benchmarks by name — and the market does not supply them

City of Hope sees a conversion rate with no context and asks whether it is good. Meanwhile peer benchmarking exists in the market in four shapes — consultant cohorts, annual studies, quarterly reports, and one unverifiable in-product claim — but no vendor evidences real-time, per-org comparison inside the workflow, and none has an answer for an organization sitting below its peers. The benchmarking engine was built against that gap.

"Prove it worked" is both the customer ask and the market's blind spot

Vine Maple Place turned Intelligent Ask on and could not tell whether it helped. Cedars-Sinai had its worst Giving Tuesday after two simultaneous changes and could not attribute it. Independently, all nine competitors publish trust and explainability language, and not one offers lift an organization can check in its own numbers. Explanation is everywhere; verification is nowhere.

Attribution is where the customer pain becomes an engineering problem

96% of Salvation Army transactions carry no referral data. FMSC cannot export the UTM parameters in its own links. Source codes drop during P2P setup and at site boundaries. That is the customer-visible symptom of what the Instrumentation and UTM projects address — which is why those two sit outside this group but belong to the same argument.

One caution about reading these together. The interviews are qualitative and recruited, the competitive scan is vendor marketing rather than verified behaviour, and the benchmarking engine runs on a single CRM export snapshot. Agreement between three sources with three different weaknesses is encouraging, not proof. Each page states its own limits under what it does not cover, and those sections are the ones to read before quoting any of this.
