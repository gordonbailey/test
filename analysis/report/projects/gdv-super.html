The problem

Donors who give more than once produce 39% of the money from 20% of the people, and get exactly the same experience as someone giving for the first time. Nobody owns them, nothing recognizes them, nothing brings them back. Most consumer businesses spend disproportionately on their best customers. This one does not.

The cheapest thing to fix is the first one: nobody is accountable for this group, so nobody notices when it shrinks.

What came out of it

The expensive ideas can only reach the smallest group

Awards, events and personal service only work at small scale. This is the biggest audience they can reach.

The 2-gift band

4.9M donors$870.9M49% of super donor GDV

Nearly three times the money from twelve times the people. Reaching them takes product and email, which cost less per person, not more.

Aim the program at the second gift. That is the moment a donor becomes valuable. It happens about 23 days after the first, and 4.9 million people are already sitting right on that line.

The Giving Conference, funded as research

How to run it as research rather than a party
How to run it as research rather than a party
What it buys

Nobody knows why 7.1M people came back

We have no research at all on why ordinary donors give. The 39 interviews were with nonprofit staff, not donors. Getting repeat givers in a room and asking them is the cheapest way to close that gap.

What it cannot do

Reach more than ~403,000 people

An event cannot reach 4.9 million people. Judged on money raised it looks like a bad investment. Judged on what it teaches every email and ad we write afterwards, it is cheap.

1Convene super donors in person, at modest scale.
2Listen, through proper interviews rather than a stage show. Why they gave, and what would make them give again.
3Feed it back into the emails, the cause suggestions, and the channels that already work.
4Measure whether the group we changed does better than the group we did not. This step is what makes it research rather than a party.

Giving Wrapped

Spotify Wrapped works because people want to post it. Applied to giving it is the cheapest lasting reward available, and a card people share is by definition person-to-person — the channel that already brings back twice as many donors.

What the donor sees

  • "You gave to 5 causes this year."
  • "You were in the top 3% of donors to animal welfare."
  • "You showed up for 4 emergencies within 48 hours."
  • "You and 812 others funded Maria's surgery."

How to measure it

A yearly recap cannot bring in second gifts, because that decision happens in 23 days. Judge it on how often people share it and whether they come back next year.

  • Opt-out for private givers. Anonymity is a real preference in giving, unlike in music.
  • Be careful with comparisons. "Top 3%" must not turn into a leaderboard built on somebody else's tragedy.

What it does not cover

Nothing here shows whether recognition actually works

Measuring where the money is is an argument about size, not evidence that status and awards fail. The most loyal donors may also be worth far more than their donations suggest, through advocacy and word of mouth. Nobody has measured that.

Cost is missing from both sides

An awards program for 403,000 people and an email program for 4.9 million cost very different amounts, and neither is estimated. An expensive idea reaching a small group can still return more per dollar.

Nobody has counted how many stop giving

How many repeat donors lapse each year, and how many come back, is unmeasured. That is half the owner's job, and the half that holds no matter which ideas get built.

"Valuable" here means frequent, not generous

Two gifts of $20 counts. One gift of $5,000 does not. Average gift falls as frequency rises, so these may be two different groups of people and a program built for one may not serve the other.

Consumer only, and that could cause friction

None of this covers donors who give through GoFundMe Pro nonprofits, or how those nonprofits would feel about us building relationships with donors they consider theirs.

None of it proves cause

Every number describes what donors already do, not what any of these ideas would change.
