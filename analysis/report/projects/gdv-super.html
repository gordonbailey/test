The problem

Donors who give more than once a year produce 39% of GDV from 20% of the donor base and get the same experience as a first-time donor, whose average gift is $94.63. No owner, no lifecycle engagement, no recognition. Every consumer business invests disproportionately in its top decile. This one does not.

The cheapest part to fix is the first: nobody is accountable for this cohort, so nobody is watching when its share drifts.

The Giving Conference, funded as research

What it buys

Nobody knows why 7.1M people came back

There is no qualitative evidence on consumer donor motivation anywhere in the customer intelligence work. The 39-interview study covers nonprofit admins, not donors. Convening repeat givers and asking them why is the cheapest way to close the largest evidence gap in this branch.

What it cannot do

Reach more than ~403,000 people

High-touch convening does not scale to the 4.9M in the 2-gift band. Judged on incremental GDV it looks like a poor investment. Judged on what it teaches lifecycle copy, cause matching and acquisition creative, it is cheap.

1Convene super donors in person, at modest scale.
2Listen through structured interviews rather than a stage show. Why they give, and what would make them give again.
3Feed it in to lifecycle copy, cause matching, and the acquisition channels that already retain.
4Measure whether the cohort informed by it converts better than the holdout. Step 4 is what makes it research.

Giving Wrapped

Spotify Wrapped works because it is a status object people want to post. Applied to generosity it is the cheapest durable reward available, and a shareable card is an interpersonal channel by construction — the category that already retains at twice baseline.

What the donor sees

  • "You gave to 5 causes this year."
  • "You were in the top 3% of donors to animal welfare."
  • "You showed up for 4 emergencies within 48 hours."
  • "You and 812 others funded Maria's surgery."

How to measure it

An annual recap cannot be the conversion mechanic, because the conversion window is 23 days. Its KPIs are share rate, opt-in to a visible tier, and next-year retention among recipients.

  • Opt-out for private givers. Anonymity is a real preference in giving, unlike in music.
  • Comparative framing needs care. "Top 3%" must not read as a leaderboard on somebody else's tragedy.

What it does not cover

Nothing here measures whether recognition works

Sizing the tail is an argument about where the dollars are, not evidence that status mechanics fail. Recognition may drive retention in the tail, and the tail may carry strategic value out of proportion to its GDV through advocacy and word of mouth. None of that has been measured.

Cost is absent from both sides

An awards program for 403,000 people and a lifecycle program for 4.9M have very different cost structures, and neither is estimated. A high-touch mechanic reaching a small pot can still return more per dollar. The comparison here is on revenue potential only.

Lapse and reactivation are unmeasured

Nobody has counted how many super donors lapse each year or what fraction come back. That is the retention half of the owner's mandate, and it is the part of the case that holds regardless of what any single mechanic does.

Frequency is the only definition of value in play

The cohort is 2+ gifts in 365 days, so a $5,000 one-time donor is not a super donor. Average gift size falls as frequency rises, so high-frequency and high-value may be different populations, and a program built for one may not serve the other.

Consumer only

All figures are from GFM v2 Prod. Nothing accounts for donors who give through GoFundMe Pro nonprofits, or for how a consumer-side program would interact with donor relationships those nonprofits consider theirs.

No causal evidence for any mechanic

Every number describes what donor populations already do, not what an intervention moves.
