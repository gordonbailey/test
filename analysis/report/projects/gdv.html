The problem

Donations are getting bigger while the number of donors stays flat. The platform is getting better at collecting from moments of generosity, not at making more generous people. So: is there a group of donors worth building a program around, and which one?

The number to quote is 20 and 39 together. Repeat donors are 20% of donors and 39% of the money. The 39% on its own means nothing. Paired with the 20%, it is the whole argument.

What was done

Every figure comes from Amplitude, over the last twelve months, using the definition already in use: a super donor is anyone who gave two or more times in 365 days. Donor counts are annual, worked out from gift counts. The result landed within 3.7% of the existing internal figure, which is a reasonable check that the query is right.

The more often someone gives, the smaller each gift. $94.63 for a one-time donor, $77.27 for someone giving five times. They give more per year and less per gift. Two things follow:

1. If this program works, average donation size will go down. Anyone watching that number will read the win as a loss. Agree it in advance.
2. "Donation sizes are up 28%" may be partly a mix effect. One-time donors give the biggest single gifts, so having more of them raises the average without anyone becoming more generous.

What one more gift is worth

Getting someone from one gift to two adds $84.37 a year. There are 29.2 million people who have given exactly once.

Aim at the rate, not the group. How many one-time donors give again is something you can measure and change: 29 million people, 12.3% do it today, and it happens within about three weeks. See acquiring better donors.

What it does not cover

Three different totals are in circulation

The deck says $1.74B, the spreadsheet says $1.55B, this page's query says $1.775B. The spreadsheet is about 13% low in every group, which points at one systematic difference rather than an error — most likely a different date range, or gross against net. The percentages hold across all three, so the 20/39 split is safe. Settle the dollar totals to one source before any of them goes in a decision document.

Counting donors annually rather than monthly changes the headline

Amplitude's default gives a monthly average, and on that basis repeat donors look like 37% of donors rather than 20% — because someone who gives twice a year is more likely to show up in any given month. The annual count is the right one here.

The totals may mix currencies

Three currency fields exist on the donation event, and I could not confirm the one I summed is already converted. Percentages survive this. Dollar totals might not.

One number in the table is an estimate

The "6+ gifts" group has no fixed gift count, so its 403,000 donors and $751 per donor are worked out from an average of 9.7 gifts each. Every other row is exact.

Consumer GoFundMe only

None of this includes GoFundMe Pro, so it says nothing about nonprofit-side donors or how the two overlap.

"Valuable" here means frequent, not generous

Two gifts of $20 makes you a super donor. One gift of $5,000 does not. That works for building habits and fails completely for major gifts.

None of this proves cause

People who give more often are worth more. Nothing here shows that getting someone to give again produces that value — the groups may simply contain different kinds of people. The $24.6M figures size a gap we can see, not an effect anyone has measured.

Pick this up next

1
Settle on one number
$1.55B, $1.74B, $1.775B. Pick the date range and the field, write them down, and redo all three from that source.
2
Aim the program at the second gift
$871M and 4.9 million people, against $303M and 403,000 among the loyal regulars. See super donor relationships.
3
Warn people that average gift size will fall
It drops at every step up in frequency. Tell whoever owns that number before the program starts, not after.
4
Design the experiment before the program
Hold a group back and compare. That is what turns the $84 into a number you can defend.
